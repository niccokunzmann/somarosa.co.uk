## What Does It Mean for a Man to Be Truly Present in Intimacy?

We often talk about intimacy in terms of confidence, attraction, sexual chemistry and performance. There is plenty of advice about becoming a better lover, building confidence and creating a satisfying sex life.

There is another aspect of intimacy that receives less attention: the ability to be present with another person while remaining connected to yourself. 

To be present with another person means we see and feel where they soften and open, and when they tense and hold. These can be very subtle, and attuning to each of these is what makes you a better lover. 

To be present with yourself involves recognising what is happening within us, understanding our needs, communicating honestly and remaining open to the experience in the present moment, instead of attaching or driving towards a specific goal. It goes beyond knowing what to do. It asks us to become more aware of how we experience connection.

## The pressure to perform

Many men grow up with messages about what masculinity should look like. Be strong. Be confident. Take the lead. Know what you are doing. Do not show too much vulnerability.

These expectations can influence how men approach relationships and sexuality. Intimacy can become something to succeed at, rather than an experience to participate in, or even surrender into.

For some men, this means focusing on whether they are performing well, whether their partner is satisfied or whether they are meeting an expectation. Attention becomes directed towards the outcome rather than the experience itself.

There is nothing inherently wrong with wanting to be a considerate, confident or attentive lover. The difficulty arises when the pressure to perform makes it harder to notice what is actually happening within us, and when we can become so focused on getting something right that we lose contact with the experience we are having.

## Understanding the body, not just the mind

We often think of emotional awareness as being able to identify and talk about our feelings. This is important, but our emotional experience is not limited to our thoughts.

We might notice tension in the chest, a tightening in the stomach, a change in our breathing or an urge to withdraw. Sometimes these physical responses appear before we have found the words to describe what we are feeling. 

Developing somatic awareness means becoming more attentive to these signals the body shows us.

In intimacy, this can help us recognise when we feel comfortable, when we are anxious, when we need to slow down or when something does not feel right. It can also help us become more aware of our boundaries, our needs and communicate them with greater clarity.

Rather than treating the body as something that needs to perform, we can begin to experience it as an important source of information.

For many people, vulnerability can feel uncomfortable, and can become something we resist and draw away from. To be truly seen, we need to show the parts of us that are tender, insecure and can feel messy.

For men who have learned to associate strength with emotional restraint, expressing uncertainty, tenderness or fear may feel unfamiliar. It means developing enough self-awareness to be honest about what is happening, rather than automatically hiding, withdrawing or pretending.

Vulnerability does not mean sharing everything immediately. It requires trust in the body for the nervous system to feel safe that when we open those vulnerable parts they won’t receive our deepest fears - to be judged, pushed away or exiled. Trust and intimacy dance in titration together. 


## Moving beyond performance

In my work with somatic practices and Tao Tantric Arts, I explore the relationship between breath, sensation, awareness and connection.

One of the things I find valuable about these approaches is that they invite us to slow down and pay attention to what’s happening beneath the surface. In the hustle of busy lives, it is easy to miss what the body is trying to communicate, sometimes for years.

Taking a few moments to become aware of the breath can help bring attention back to the body. Noticing areas of tension, recognising changes in sensation and allowing ourselves to pause can all support a more conscious relationship with our experience. 

In intimate situations, this might mean communicating when we want to slow down, noticing whether we are genuinely comfortable, or becoming curious about what we are experiencing rather than judging it.

It also means recognising that intimacy is not simply about sexual activity. It can be found in eye contact, conversation, affection, listening, touch and the ability to be with another person without feeling that we need to prove anything.

These practices are not a formula for perfect relationships. They are invitations to develop awareness and make more conscious choices.
